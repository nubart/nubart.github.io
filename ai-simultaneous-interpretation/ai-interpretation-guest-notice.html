Can AI Interpretation Make Serious Mistakes? What to Tell Your Guests and Your Client



In more than 30 years as a human interpreter, no client ever asked me what would happen if I made a serious mistake. Now I run Nubart TRANSLATE, an AI simultaneous interpretation system for live events, and event organizers ask me exactly that: if the AI makes a serious mistake at an event we run for a client, where does that leave us? Yes, AI interpretation can make serious mistakes. So can human interpreters, only different ones. This article explains where AI mistakes come from, how to address them before an event, and what to include in your agreement with clients.

Human and AI interpreters make different mistakes

Interpreters make mistakes. We mishear a number, choose the wrong word, or miss the end of a long sentence. Listeners can hear the difference: an unsure human interpreter hesitates or speaks quickly to catch up with the speaker. However, an AI voice sounds equally confident whether the translation is correct or not. Therefore, a wrong word is harder to spot, especially for listeners who don't understand the original language. I describe the other differences in AI versus human interpretation.

Where AI mistakes come from

Most errors occur when the AI does not “understand” the speakers correctly.

The terminology for the event has not been prepared

Most mistakes start before the translation begins. If speech recognition mishears something, the translation will be correct but refer to the wrong word. Consider a brand name. The speaker says "Nike," but pronounces it in a way that the system does not recognize and does not associate with the brand. The result will probably be incorrect every time the speaker says it. Preparation helps. If the brand name is included in your documents, Nubart will add it to the preparation and it will usually be recognized. However, nobody can foresee every pronunciation, and no preparation can cover everything. Why a name can go wrong even when it was in your documents, I explain in Why AI Interpretation Still Gets Names Wrong After Preparation.

Bad isolation of the speaker's voice

An AI live translation system attempts to make sense of everything it hears. Therefore, if it is picking up sounds from the audience or ambient noise in addition to the speaker, it will mix all of these sound sources together and transcribe them as if they were a single voice. Naturally, the transcription and subsequent translation will be incorrect.

To prevent this, it is essential to properly isolate the speaker's voice using a good directional microphone.

Speakers who are hard to understand

The same thing happens when the speech itself is difficult to understand. An accent is only one cause. Others include mumbling, frequent use of "um," very fast speech, very long sentences, and a low voice. Speech recognition technology struggles with all of these issues, as do human interpreters. There is no fix during the speech.

There is also a social aspect. No one would ask a famous athlete to speak clearly or to avoid his accent, since this could affect the quality of the translation. A human interpreter cannot repair lack of clarity, and neither can an AI.

What helps before the event

You will seldom be able to test with the actual speakers. A head of government will not give you 30 minutes of their time, and at an international conference with 20 speakers, you may not know most of them. What you can do is read and understand our instructions, and then test under the same technical conditions as the event. Use the same microphones and AV setup in the actual room with a colleague speaking. Then, at least, no technical issues will make recognition more difficult. During the sound check, observe the audio quality indicator on the speaker's screen. It shows how clearly the speech recognition software can understand the speaker. A low value can come from the equipment, but also from mumbling or a strong accent, so it works as an early warning.

Nubart offers a free test for up to 30 minutes for this. The free test does not include event preparation. If you want to hear your own terminology prepared, ask about the paid prepared test. Testing creates some confidence, but it does not remove the risk. A speaker with a cold can pronounce a word differently on the day.

Send Nubart your agenda, presentations, technical papers and a list of speakers, including the names and brands that will come up. Nubart prepares the terminology for your event from them, much like briefing a human interpreter. This improves the chance that names and terms come out right. It does not guarantee it. A microphone close to the speaker helps more than most organizers expect.

What to tell your guests and your own client

Your guests

One visible sentence is enough, in the place where guests choose their language: on the event page or next to the QR code. For example:

Interpretation at this event is provided by AI. It may contain errors, is not certified and has no legal force. For the exact wording, the spoken original applies.

The Terms of Nubart TRANSLATE require customers to tell participants that the interpretation is provided by AI. A sentence like this does that.

Institutions have used the same idea for human interpreters for decades. The United Nations, the European Parliament and the German Bundestag publish a disclaimer saying that interpretation serves communication and that only the original speech is authentic.

Do you also need a privacy notice for your guests? Listeners use Nubart TRANSLATE without registering or giving any personal data, so the AI sentence above is all they need. Speakers are different: they log in, and what they say is transcribed. As the event organizer, you are responsible for informing participants, usually in the privacy notice you already have for your event.

Your own client

If you organize the event for a client, invite the client to the test. Then they hear for themselves what AI interpretation sounds like, and their expectations come from experience, not from a paper.

Also: promise the effort, not the result. You can promise AI interpretation that is prepared with the material your client sends you and tested under the conditions of the event. Do not promise a level of accuracy. The Terms of Nubart TRANSLATE do not guarantee one either, so you could not pass such a guarantee on.

Put this into your offer or contract in plain words. For example:

The interpretation is provided by an AI system (Nubart TRANSLATE). It is prepared with the material you send us and tested under the conditions of the event. It may contain errors and omissions, and we do not guarantee a specific accuracy. Where the exact wording of a statement matters, the spoken original applies.

If parts of the event could have serious legal, medical, financial or safety consequences, add that you recommend human interpreters for those parts. Your client signs the offer or contract anyway, so a separate form is not needed. A clause like this sets expectations. It cannot take away rights that the law gives your client.

When a human interpreter is the better choice

Plan for human interpreters for those parts of the event where an error could have serious legal, medical, financial, or safety consequences. Examples include binding negotiations, signing agreements, and safety briefings. The Terms of Nubart TRANSLATE exclude certain uses, such as emergency services and clinical decisions.
